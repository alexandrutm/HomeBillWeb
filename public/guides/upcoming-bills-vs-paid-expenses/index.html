Upcoming bills versus expenses already paid

An upcoming bill tells you what you expect to pay and when. A recorded expense tells you what you have entered as spent. Keeping those views distinct makes a monthly review easier to interpret.

Published November 5, 2026 · 4 min read · By the HomeBill team

An upcoming bill is a plan or expectation

A recurring rent payment or a utility bill with a future due date belongs in the list of expected costs. Its amount may be confirmed or estimated. A scheduled bill is useful for seeing what is coming, but it does not by itself prove that the payment has been made.

A paid expense is a recorded transaction

Once you have paid a bill, record the expense using the amount and date shown by your payment or statement. If the final amount differs from the estimate, the transaction should reflect the actual amount you paid, not the old forecast.

Why counting both can mislead

If you add an upcoming bill and a paid transaction as though they were two separate expenses, you can count the same cost twice. When reviewing a month, check which figures represent forecasts and which represent transactions already recorded. Use the actual payment to understand spending, and the upcoming-bill list to plan for what remains due.

Quick example: You estimate an electricity bill at €70. The final bill is €76 and you pay it. Keep the expected amount and payment status current, and record €76 as the expense. Do not treat €70 and €76 as two separate payments.

A simple monthly review

  1. Review income and expenses you actually recorded for the month.
  2. Check upcoming or overdue bills separately, including any amounts still estimated.
  3. Update bills after payment and correct estimates when a statement arrives.
  4. Look ahead to annual or less-frequent bills due in the coming months.
